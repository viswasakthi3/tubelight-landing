# Landing Page Design Intelligence

An evidence-informed operating book for AI designers

Consumer software • Communication • Interface design • Qualified conversion

Edition 1 • 12 November 2026

## Start here: the operating contract

Design a page that helps a relevant visitor recognize a useful outcome, understand how the software produces it, judge whether the promise is believable, and complete an appropriate next action. Continue the promise into signup and first use. Optimize qualified conversion subject to truthful communication, accessible operation, and acceptable downstream outcomes.

This is a decision manual for Claude Design and other design or development agents. It is not a claim about the current capabilities or interface of any particular AI tool. Supply it alongside a product fact sheet and actual product assets. The agent must turn those facts into a communication script before selecting components. A beautiful screenshot is only one intermediate deliverable.

Use the book selectively. A returning visitor looking for a download does not need the same explanation as someone discovering an unfamiliar AI workflow. Retrieve the relevant insight cards, inspect their boundaries, and justify the resulting design. Do not apply every rule to every page.

### What maximum conversion means

For a live product, choose a primary outcome such as a completed signup or purchase per eligible assigned visitor. Pair it with a meaningful activation outcome in a defined window. A completed account that never reaches useful output is not equivalent to a successful customer. For a waitlist, distinguish a submitted address, any required verification, an invitation delivered, and eventual activation. Do not invent immediate activation for an unreleased product.

Define eligibility before exposure using defensible criteria such as supported geography, platform, or acquisition campaign. Do not improve the reported rate by excluding visitors who abandon, fail to load the page, or never watch the demo after seeing a treatment. Report these failures. A page can appropriately help someone determine that the product is unsuitable; concealment is not an optimization strategy.

The operating objective is a constrained business decision, not a universal psychological equation. Choose the smallest benefit worth implementing, the risks that would block rollout, and the observation window before testing. If more registrations coincide with more misconceptions, refunds, or unusable accounts, investigate the trade-off rather than declaring an automatic win.

### How to use this book in an AI workflow

1. Read Part A and complete the product and visitor records in Part C.
2. Retrieve a small, relevant set of Part B insight cards by the observed uncertainty.
3. Write the communication script in Part C. Decide what each visual must demonstrate.
4. Apply Part D to the responsive UI and its complete interaction states.
5. Use the master prompt in Part E to produce a traceable implementation specification.
6. Use Part F for diagnosis, instrumentation, and real-user validation.
7. Consult Part G for worked examples. Treat their facts and copy as fictional, not reusable product claims.
8. Follow source IDs into the evidence register. Preserve uncertainty when copying a rule.

## Part A — Research and the visitor decision model

### A1. Research approach and evidence boundaries

The evidence foundation combines the 36 research and methodological entries in the attention and landing-page report with 15 additional research entries on grouping, mental models, technology acceptance, interaction laws, framing, narrative, AI interaction, pricing, and perceived waiting. Usability guidance, standards, and the Google delay experiment remain separately labeled. The new synthesis is a structured narrative review, not an exhaustive systematic review or a meta-analysis of landing-page conversion.

The research questions are practical: what helps people notice relevant information, form an accurate model of software, evaluate value and risk, operate the interface, and obtain the promised result? Sources were selected for direct relevance, methodological transparency, useful counterevidence, or a mechanism that can be tested locally. Publisher pages, author manuscripts, institutional research pages, and standards bodies take precedence over unattributed marketing summaries.

The original report's access labels are retained for S01–S36. New searches on 12 November 2026 used source families covering Gestalt grouping, mental models in device learning, technology acceptance, Hick–Hyman and Fitts models, behavior models, loss framing, expertise reversal, human-AI guidelines, narrative transportation, ego-depletion replication, price salience, and operational transparency. Current W3C and web.dev guidance was checked for implementation details. Full-text sections were inspected where accessible; abstract-only entries are explicitly identified. No inaccessible method, missing participant count, or effect estimate has been filled in by inference.

Exclude claims supported only by attractive example sites, unverified uplift screenshots, testimonials about a tactic, model-generated heatmaps, or unexplained neuroscience language. A commercial case study without a denominator, comparator, dates, assignment procedure, and outcome definition can suggest a hypothesis but cannot establish a reliable causal lift. Keep failed searches and uncertain applicability visible in the evidence record.

For each source ask: what changed, who participated, what was measured, how assignment worked, and what could otherwise explain the result? Distinguish a stimulus count from a participant count, repeated observations from independent people, purchase intention from purchase, and page scrolling from understanding. A large dataset does not remove selection bias. A laboratory experiment can establish a task effect while leaving voluntary consumer adoption unknown.

### A2. Evidence labels the AI must preserve

| Label | Meaning | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|
| Strong, within scope | Convergent or well-controlled support for the specified outcome | Use as a mechanism or task rationale; still qualify transfer |
| Moderate | Relevant evidence with meaningful setting, access, or design limits | Prefer a conditional design hypothesis and local test |
| Preliminary | Narrow or early evidence, limited replication or indirect outcome | Explore when relevant; avoid presenting as a default winner |
| Hypothesis | Reasoned application without direct local validation | Label it and state what observation would change the decision |
| Usability guidance | Established practitioner principles | Use for inspection; do not invent an experimental effect size |
| Standard | Normative technical requirement or recommendation | Implement and verify the applicable criteria; not conversion proof |
| Industry experiment | Primary operational evidence outside the peer-reviewed register | Preserve setting and reporting limitations |

Confidence in a finding and confidence in a conversion effect are separate fields. Most cards have stronger support for their narrow mechanism than for a software acquisition effect. Priority is also separate from confidence: a broken submit button has immediate practical priority even without a paper estimating its exact revenue loss.

Priority codes used here: P0 means a blocker when the stated condition exists; P1 means an early design or research priority; P2 means a refinement after more fundamental uncertainty is resolved. These are editorial triage categories, not measured impact rankings.

### A3. Synthesis: what is supported, conditional, and unsettled

Attention is selective and goal-dependent. Salience can help discovery, but a conspicuous element need not answer the visitor's question. Early aesthetic impressions are not equivalent to understanding a workflow. Memory and integration demands matter: viewers must connect the relevant input, operation, and result rather than reconstruct them from disconnected fragments. [S01–S09]

Instructional research supports selective cueing, spatial and temporal integration, and meaningful segmentation. Its outcome is often learning, not voluntary viewing or signup. Advertising and reading studies add engagement evidence but use different measurements: gaze, switching away, synchronized looking, or scrolling. These should not be combined into one metric called attention. [S08–S12; S31–S36]

Choice overload is conditional. A broad average can conceal moderators, while a familiar interaction law can be misapplied outside its original task. Neither establishes a universal one-CTA page. Curiosity and emotional needs can matter, but their presence does not establish demand for a product. [S13; S15–S17; S40; S42]

Task-level evidence on registration forms and accessibility is especially useful for removing operational barriers. Trust requires information about the actual uncertainty. Authentic proof and usable privacy information serve different purposes from decorative reassurance. [S25–S28]

Counterevidence is part of the design system. More minimal is not always more understandable; a creative structure differs from perceptual clutter. Attractive appearance does not reliably repair poor usability. Urgency effects vary across products and online contexts. Faster service and perceived service value are related in ways that depend on the task and what the interface reveals. [S14–S16; S19–S20; S30; S35; S50]

Price disclosure research is an important warning against equating sales with informed choice. A large ticket-marketplace experiment found that later fee disclosure increased purchases and spending. The measured success of such a tactic is not a reason to conceal software costs. Keep total commitment understandable and evaluate informed uptake. [S49]

### A4. A practical model of the visitor

Use six questions as a diagnostic map, not a fixed sequence or a claim that six mental stages have been experimentally established. A visitor may jump to price, revisit proof, compare another site, or act immediately.

| Visitor question | Possible feeling to investigate | What the page must make available |
|---|---|---|
| Is this relevant to my situation? | Recognition, indifference, irritation at a mismatch | A recognizable task, outcome, and audience fit |
| What does it actually do? | Curiosity or confusion | A concrete explanation and a faithful example |
| Will it work for me? | Hope, skepticism, uncertainty | Conditions, compatibility, mechanisms, representative evidence |
| Is the benefit worth the change? | Desire, reluctance, attachment to an existing method | Useful outcome compared with actual setup and ongoing effort |
| Can I trust this commitment? | Caution about money, data, access, or reputation | Specific answers about the relevant risk |
| Can I do it and get the result? | Confidence, frustration, relief | A usable action, feedback, recovery, and first-use path |

Treat feelings as hypotheses until supported by interviews, observation, or appropriately collected self-report. A pause over a form may reflect uncertainty, distraction, accessibility trouble, or a slow connection. Do not label it fear from analytics alone. A visitor who leaves may have rejected the offer, found what they needed, switched devices, or encountered an error.

The earlier shorthand of designing for a distracted or "drunk" user should become a respectful operating constraint: support limited attention, unfamiliarity, interruption, and varied access needs. Do not recruit impaired participants as a substitute for representative research. Do not remove meaningful decisions or treat visitors as incapable. Make operation easy while preserving informed judgment.

## Part B — The actionable insight library

### Retrieval index and conflict handling

I01–I06 address relevance and attention. I07–I13 address comprehension and memory. I14–I19 address motivation, feeling, and evidence. I20–I27 address conversion commitments and usable action. I28–I34 address demonstrations, mobile presentation, and visual identity. I35–I40 address accessibility, performance, measurement, and continued product value.

Start with the observed failure. If users cannot explain the product, retrieve I07–I13 before testing urgency. If they understand but hesitate over file access, retrieve I18 and I25. If willing users fail to submit, retrieve I22, I24, and I35. If a video attracts looking but creates wrong beliefs, retrieve I28–I31 and I39.

Resolve conflicts in this order: product truth and informed commitment; ability to complete the task; correct understanding; relevant motivation and proof; distinctive presentation; optional polish. This is an operating policy, not a universal ranking of psychological effects. A contradiction requires a contextual choice, not an average of slogans. When brevity removes critical meaning, retain the meaning. When a larger CTA obscures content, fix hierarchy without obstructing reading. When motion explains a transformation but delays the page, provide a lightweight static path.

Every card below contains the requested principle, behavior, evidence, strength, applicability, implementation, example, mistake, trade-off, validation, and priority. Examples are hypothetical. Their wording is not evidence of a measured lift.


### I01 — Make relevance recognizable

Principle: give prominence to the outcome the intended visitor is trying to identify.

Human behavior and evidence: attention depends on goals and meaning as well as visual contrast. S01 and S02 support this mechanism; the application to acquisition is indirect. Strength: strong general rationale, moderate transfer, local conversion untested.

Applicability: unfamiliar products, cold traffic, and campaigns promising a specific result. Implementation: place a recognizable task or output beside the product category and primary action. Use real user language from discovery. Example: a duplicate-photo tool shows two duplicate groups and the proposed selection, rather than an abstract glowing sphere.

Common mistake and trade-off: making the largest object the least informative one; overfitting the hero to a narrow use case can exclude other valuable audiences. State the primary case and provide an honest route to additional uses.

Validation: ask visitors what situation the product is for before explaining it. Compare recognition and qualified action by pre-existing traffic segment. Priority: P1; P0 if the hero currently implies the wrong product.

### I02 — Preserve the acquisition promise

Principle: continue the claim that caused the visit into the landing page and first useful result.

Human behavior and evidence: information scent and prior goals provide a rationale for coherent expectations. S01, S51, and G04 support the reasoning, not a numerical message-match uplift. Strength: usability guidance plus local hypothesis.

Applicability: ad campaigns, creator referrals, search, email, and repeat visits. Implementation: record the referring creative, its exact promise, and what is already known. A campaign about removing duplicate photos should land on that workflow, even if the product also edits videos.

Common mistake and trade-off: rewriting the hero into a broader company slogan after a specific promise; excessive personalization can fragment maintenance and conceal inconsistencies. Create variants only for meaningful intent differences, with shared verified facts.

Validation: ask what visitors expected before arriving and what they believe now. Test matched routes with consistent assignment and outcomes. Priority: P1 when traffic sources communicate different offers.

### I03 — Use hierarchy to guide a useful first look

Principle: make the main claim, evidence, and next action distinguishable without making everything loud.

Human behavior and evidence: S04 examines fast aesthetic judgments; S05 distinguishes attention to image, text, and brand. Neither establishes a universal hero arrangement. Strength: moderate for the studied visual outcomes; implementation is a hypothesis.

Applicability: dense first screens and competing promotional messages. Implementation: define the intended first takeaway, secondary explanation, and available action. Adjust scale, spacing, contrast, and alignment together. Example: a readable output preview carries the explanation while a single accent treatment identifies the primary action.

Common mistake and trade-off: treating all elements as equally important or suppressing necessary conditions through faint type. Prominence must not make qualifications illegible.

Validation: use first-impression recall and task-based discovery; inspect whether people find both the main benefit and material conditions. Priority: P1, after verifying the message itself.

### I04 — Group information according to its relationship

Principle: the visual grouping should match the meaning users need to infer.

Human behavior and evidence: the Gestalt review S37 describes proximity, similarity, connectedness, and figure–ground organization. Strength: strong perceptual foundation; the proposed page layout remains untested.

Applicability: input–output examples, pricing, proof, captions, and form help. Implementation: keep a metric with its unit and measurement conditions; align comparable plan attributes; place a caption with the object it explains. Example: attach “PDF export” to the actual exported document, not to an unrelated illustration.

Common mistake and trade-off: placing a limitation nearer a different plan, or using identical styling for actions and inert labels. Strong grouping can imply a false relationship. Check semantics as well as visual cleanliness.

Validation: ask users which claim, plan, or image a label belongs to and what is included. Priority: P1 where relationships are ambiguous; P2 for cosmetic regrouping without a known problem.

### I05 — Support scanning and direct access

Principle: let visitors find the answer they need without reading in the designer's preferred order.

Human behavior and evidence: G01 describes scanning behavior; S51 frames information seeking as value gained relative to effort. Strength: established usability rationale, not a fixed gaze law.

Applicability: pages with several evaluation questions or both novice and informed visitors. Implementation: make headings answer-bearing, add meaningful jump links when useful, and keep a direct route to pricing or download. Example: “Works with local JPG and PNG files” communicates more than “Powerful compatibility.”

Common mistake and trade-off: forcing an F-shaped layout, withholding the CTA until the final section, or adding navigation so extensive it becomes another search task. Preserve a fast path and optional depth.

Validation: give information-finding tasks and record success, misinterpretations, and unnecessary detours. Priority: P1 on long pages or mixed-intent traffic.

### I06 — Keep important information persistently available

Principle: a critical fact should not depend on catching a momentary animation.

Human behavior and evidence: S03 demonstrates task-dependent failures to notice visible events. Strength: strong laboratory phenomenon; prevalence on a particular page is unknown.

Applicability: carousels, animated pricing, demo limitations, status changes, and moving controls. Implementation: keep commitment conditions in ordinary text and provide a stable output frame. Example: show “Preview only; files are not changed until you confirm” throughout a destructive-action demo.

Common mistake and trade-off: assuming a brightly colored badge was read because it appeared on screen. Persistent detail can add clutter, so reserve it for consequential information and group it with the relevant action.

Validation: interrupt the experience at different points and ask what action will occur. Test with motion disabled and with the video skipped. Priority: P0 for missed material conditions; otherwise P1.

### I07 — Explain input, operation, output, and control

Principle: build a usable mental model, not merely recognition of a product category.

Human behavior and evidence: S38 reports that an explanatory device model helped operation when it supported inference of the required steps. Strength: moderate experimental support in an older device-learning setting; software marketing transfer is a hypothesis.

Applicability: unfamiliar categories, automation, AI, and technical products. Implementation: specify what users supply, what the system changes, what they receive, and what remains under their control. Example: “Upload a transcript, review suggested action items, then export the edited list.”

Common mistake and trade-off: “Your AI copilot for everything” leaves the operation unspecified; explaining architecture can also overwhelm visitors. Show only mechanisms needed to understand, believe, or choose the product.

Validation: ask users to predict the next step and describe what happens with a new example. Record invented capabilities separately. Priority: P1; P0 if users believe unsupported automation will occur.

### I08 — Use concrete language with defined scope

Principle: name an observable result and the conditions under which it is available.

Human behavior and evidence: S29 concerns concrete customer-service language; S36 links language and continued content consumption. Neither directly tests software hero copy. Strength: moderate adjacent evidence, local headline hypothesis.

Applicability: vague headlines, unfamiliar terms, and broad AI claims. Implementation: replace abstract adjectives with an input, action, or output. Example: “Find duplicate photos before you delete them” communicates an operation and a safeguard; “Transform your digital life” does not.

Common mistake and trade-off: becoming so specific that the sentence is unreadably technical, or inventing a precise time-saving number. Pair a plain result with optional technical detail. Keep personality in the voice without obscuring meaning.

Validation: compare unaided explanations and perceived relevance before testing conversion. Priority: P1 when people cannot paraphrase the offer.

### I09 — Show a representative, readable example

Principle: choose the visual evidence from the misunderstanding it must resolve.

Human behavior and evidence: S02, S08, and S09 motivate meaningful visual content and integrated explanation. Strength: moderate transfer from attention and learning; no universal screenshot advantage.

Applicability: products whose output can be shown faithfully. Implementation: identify the smallest complete example, crop irrelevant chrome, and retain the context needed to interpret it. Example: show one incoming receipt, its extracted fields, and an editable correction, rather than a miniature full dashboard.

Common mistake and trade-off: a fake interface can suggest unavailable capabilities; an overly cropped screenshot can hide work or conditions. Label illustrative states and material time compression. Provide authentic detail when it matters for evaluation.

Validation: test the visual alone and the combined hero with separate participants where learning would contaminate comparisons. Priority: P1 for explanation gaps.

### I10 — Reduce memory demands, not just word count

Principle: keep related information available when it must be compared.

Human behavior and evidence: S06 concerns limited memory under specified conditions; G07 recommends recognition support. Strength: strong general rationale; no fixed UI element limit follows.

Applicability: before/after comparisons, plan selection, multi-step demos, and form instructions. Implementation: preserve a small input reference while displaying the output; show field requirements near the field. Example: leave the original filename visible while showing the proposed new name.

Common mistake and trade-off: hiding every explanation behind a click to make a screenshot look minimal. Keeping everything visible can also overload the page. Retain decision-critical context and disclose optional depth on demand.

Validation: ask users to compare or operate without returning to an earlier screen; note memory-dependent errors. Priority: P1 where integration fails.

### I11 — Cue the change at the moment it matters

Principle: highlight the part of the interface that explains the result, with an adjacent label.

Human behavior and evidence: S08 and S09 support signaling and integrated explanations in learning. Strength: moderate to strong for instructional outcomes, untested acquisition transfer.

Applicability: unfamiliar workflows or visually dense output. Implementation: use one relevant highlight per explanatory beat; synchronize the label with the visible change; keep captions clear of the UI. Example: highlight the newly extracted date while the receipt line remains visible.

Common mistake and trade-off: animated rings around every feature or a narrator describing something that has already disappeared. Highlights can obscure content or imply clickability. Use a consistent non-interactive treatment.

Validation: compare reconstruction of the workflow and errors, not only gaze at the highlighted region. Priority: P1 for missed changes; P2 for an already understood demonstration.

### I12 — Adapt depth to prior knowledge

Principle: provide enough explanation for unfamiliar visitors and a faster route for experienced ones.

Human behavior and evidence: S45 reviews how instructional effects vary with expertise. Strength: moderate instructional rationale; page sequencing is a local hypothesis.

Applicability: developer tools, specialist software, or mixed search and social traffic. Implementation: establish the plain-language outcome, then expose a concise technical path. Example: a data tool offers “See an example” and a clearly separate “Read the API schema” link.

Common mistake and trade-off: assuming all users need a beginner tutorial or all know the category vocabulary. Too many branches create navigation burden. Use observed intent and task knowledge, not demographic stereotypes, to decide which routes matter.

Validation: recruit both relevant novices and experienced users; report their misunderstandings and task outcomes separately. Priority: P1 when one explanation demonstrably fails a major audience.

### I13 — Simplify while preserving informed decisions

Principle: remove avoidable interpretation effort while retaining consequential facts.

Human behavior and evidence: G02 and G03 support clear purpose, familiar choices, and reduced unnecessary effort. Strength: usability guidance, not a conversion theorem.

Applicability: all pages, especially trials, subscriptions, and data-sensitive software. Implementation: place the core action and commitment conditions together; put supporting detail in clearly labeled disclosure. Example: “Start a 14-day trial” sits beside the verified payment requirement and what occurs when the trial ends.

Common mistake and trade-off: removing price, renewal, or compatibility because it disrupts a minimal hero. More words are justified when they prevent a consequential false expectation. A concise summary can link to complete terms.

Validation: ask what users expect to pay, receive, provide, and do next. Priority: P0 for misleading omissions; P1 for unnecessary explanation burden.

### I14 — Connect the outcome to a real use occasion

Principle: explain why this result matters in a recognizable situation.

Human behavior and evidence: S39 finds associations between perceived usefulness, ease, and technology usage measures. Strength: moderate correlational support, not a causal headline result.

Applicability: products with a clear capability but weak perceived relevance. Implementation: connect the feature to the visitor's current job and alternative. Example: “Prepare the photos you want to keep before moving to a smaller laptop” provides a situation for duplicate cleanup.

Common mistake and trade-off: translating every feature into the same vague “save time” benefit. A strong use occasion may be narrow. Offer additional examples only when evidence indicates another important audience.

Validation: ask about recent occurrences of the problem and what users actually did. Compare relevance and adoption, not agreement with a hypothetical benefit. Priority: P1 before polishing persuasion.

### I15 — Treat feelings as research questions

Principle: connect emotional value to an experience the product can deliver.

Human behavior and evidence: S17 associates need fulfillment with positive product experiences; S32 studies emotion during video ads. Strength: moderate within their settings; emotional-copy effects remain uncertain.

Applicability: products promising confidence, control, expression, relief, or enjoyment. Implementation: record the frustrating situation, desired feeling, credible product mechanism, and supporting evidence. Example: an editable preview can support a sense of control more concretely than “Never worry again.”

Common mistake and trade-off: diagnosing anxiety from a pause or declaring that all users seek status. Emotional intensity can reduce trust. Use the visitor's own words and avoid amplifying shame or imagined threats.

Validation: ask neutral retrospective questions and observe whether the product experience supports the claim. Priority: P1 when motivation is unclear; P2 for tone refinements after relevance works.

### I16 — Use curiosity to reveal a relevant answer

Principle: create an interesting question whose answer clarifies the product.

Human behavior and evidence: S13 concerns information seeking and memory in trivia; S36 separates sustained consumption from comprehension. Strength: suggestive for a marketing reveal.

Applicability: visible transformations, surprising but genuine results, and optional demos. Implementation: establish the product category, show a relevant outcome, and reveal the mechanism promptly. Example: show a messy set of notes beside the editable plan, then demonstrate how one task was extracted.

Common mistake and trade-off: concealing what the product is until the end or exaggerating the result to earn a click. A reveal can attract attention while wasting a visitor's time.

Validation: measure stopping or play separately from correct understanding and downstream action. Priority: P2 unless the main problem is failure to recognize an otherwise relevant demonstration.

### I17 — Make proof correspond to the claim

Principle: choose evidence that answers the exact reason for doubt.

Human behavior and evidence: S18 demonstrates social influence on ratings; S27 synthesizes trust relationships. Neither means that a logo establishes product effectiveness. Strength: moderate trust rationale; proof format must be tested.

Applicability: unfamiliar brands, exceptional claims, and high-commitment actions. Implementation: use a real workflow for a functional claim, a scoped measurement for speed, and an authentic customer account for experience. Example: show the source file and resulting export for compatibility rather than an unexplained “trusted” badge.

Common mistake and trade-off: inventing testimonials, implying a customer relationship from a technology logo, or displaying results without conditions. Detailed proof takes space, so show a useful summary with access to context.

Validation: ask what the evidence establishes and what remains uncertain. Priority: P0 for unsupported claims; P1 for a verified but poorly demonstrated promise.

### I18 — Answer the actual data or permission concern

Principle: make consequential data handling understandable before commitment.

Human behavior and evidence: S28 experimentally varied visible privacy information in shopping; S27 concerns trust relationships. Strength: moderate adjacent behavioral evidence, not a universal badge effect.

Applicability: uploads, browser extensions, inbox access, local files, and personal data. Implementation: state what is accessed, why, where processing happens, and how users can stop or remove it, using verified facts. Example: “Select one folder to scan” is useful only if the product truly restricts access that way.

Common mistake and trade-off: saying “100% secure” or “private by design” in place of a concrete explanation. Excessive detail can obscure the decision, so summarize the material facts and link to precise documentation.

Validation: ask users to describe the data flow and permissions. Audit the claim against implementation. Priority: P0 for false privacy claims; P1 for unresolved access objections.

### I19 — Treat scarcity and loss framing as conditional

Principle: communicate real limitations without manufacturing urgency or assuming fear is optimal.

Human behavior and evidence: S19–S20 show context dependence in scarcity; S43–S44 concern framing and loss-related choices. Strength: moderate or strong within studied decisions, weak for a universal landing-page prescription.

Applicability: genuine cohort capacity, actual deadlines, or a verified cost of an existing workflow. Implementation: explain the constraint, consequence, and alternative. Example: “We are inviting 50 testers this month” requires a real allocation policy and does not imply access is guaranteed by joining.

Common mistake and trade-off: importing a loss-aversion coefficient as a predicted conversion multiplier or restarting a timer. Urgency can increase pressure while reducing fit or trust.

Validation: test equivalent truthful information, including trust, correct expectations, and later activation. Priority: P2 after clarity and usable action; P0 to remove deception.

### I20 — Make the CTA predict the next step

Principle: the label and nearby copy should describe the actual commitment.

Human behavior and evidence: G04 and S51 support useful information scent. Strength: established navigation rationale; specific copy remains a hypothesis.

Applicability: every primary and secondary action. Implementation: distinguish a download, account creation, waitlist, preview, trial, and sales inquiry. Example: use “Join the waitlist” when access is not available; do not use “Start creating” if the click only collects an email.

Common mistake and trade-off: choosing an emotionally forceful label that hides what happens next. Literal labels can feel less expressive, so preserve brand tone in supporting copy without changing the meaning.

Validation: ask users what happens after clicking, then inspect the actual destination and confirmation. Priority: P0 for false expectations; P1 for unclear action labels.

### I21 — Offer useful choices with a clear hierarchy

Principle: reduce uncertainty in choices rather than optimizing an arbitrary button count.

Human behavior and evidence: S15–S16 find conditional choice-overload patterns; S40 cautions against simplistic Hick-law transfer. Strength: strong reason to avoid universal rules, local configuration untested.

Applicability: preview versus signup, pricing tiers, operating-system downloads, and mixed readiness. Implementation: identify the main commitment and a genuinely useful lower-commitment route. Example: “Download for Windows” can coexist with “See the cleanup preview.”

Common mistake and trade-off: adding several synonymous CTAs or removing a needed evaluation path to obey a one-button rule. More options are justified only when they serve distinct visitor needs.

Validation: ask people to choose a route for a realistic goal and explain their choice. Measure completed actions across the assigned audience. Priority: P1 when alternatives are confusing; otherwise P2.

### I22 — Treat the form as an interaction, not a rectangle

Principle: help willing visitors submit correctly and recover without losing work.

Human behavior and evidence: S25 tests a bundle of registration-form guidelines, improving several task outcomes without isolating each element. Strength: moderate direct form evidence, voluntary signup lift unknown.

Applicability: waitlists, account creation, trial setup, and contact forms. Implementation: use persistent labels, appropriate input types, explicit requirements, useful errors, preserved valid entries, and a reliable success state. Example: an invalid address receives an adjacent correction hint while all other entries remain intact.

Common mistake and trade-off: assuming fewer fields alone solves usability. Removing necessary qualification may create downstream work; asking unnecessary information creates burden.

Validation: observe completion, retry counts, error types, and submission confirmation. Test slow networks and duplicate clicks. Priority: P0 if users cannot finish; P1 for avoidable friction.

### I23 — Make targets easy to activate accurately

Principle: reduce unnecessary motor precision and accidental actions.

Human behavior and evidence: S41 reviews Fitts's law for pointing; G09 provides current target-size guidance. Strength: strong motor rationale and normative implementation guidance, not motivational evidence.

Applicability: mobile controls, close icons, plan selectors, playback, and form buttons. Implementation: provide adequate hit areas and separation; make the intended target stable. Example: the small visible play icon can sit inside a larger properly labeled button.

Common mistake and trade-off: interpreting a larger target as a guaranteed higher desire to sign up. Oversized sticky controls can obscure reading or keyboard focus. Preserve access to content and alternative actions.

Validation: test mis-taps, keyboard operation, and target dimensions at actual viewports. Priority: P0 for inaccessible operation; P1 for repeated activation errors.

### I24 — Preserve control and explain state changes

Principle: make the current state, recoverable actions, and next step understandable.

Human behavior and evidence: G07 covers status, control, and recovery; S46 extends these concerns to uncertain AI behavior. Strength: usability guidance supported by practitioner evaluation, not a conversion trial.

Applicability: uploads, asynchronous jobs, simulated demos, errors, and navigation. Implementation: specify idle, progress, success, partial success, failure, cancellation, and retry. Example: “Upload failed. Your file has not been processed. Try again” differs from a spinner that never resolves.

Common mistake and trade-off: inventing progress percentages or automatically advancing after an unread result. Detailed status should help the decision rather than expose irrelevant implementation logs.

Validation: interrupt connections and tasks; confirm users know whether anything was submitted, charged, changed, or saved. Priority: P0 for ambiguous commitments or destructive actions; otherwise P1.

### I25 — Treat downloading as a distinct commitment

Principle: answer platform, provenance, permissions, and first-run questions before and after a download.

Human behavior and evidence: this is a design synthesis using S27–S28 and G07, not a directly measured download-lift rule. Strength: hypothesis with strong usability relevance.

Applicability: desktop utilities, extensions, installers, and companion applications. Implementation: show supported OS, the intended artifact, any account requirement, and verified installation guidance. A mobile visitor may need an explicit route to continue on desktop, rather than a misleading mobile download.

Common mistake and trade-off: counting the download-button click as successful installation, or claiming a file is signed without verifying release artifacts. Asking for an email just to access an otherwise public installer adds a separate commitment that needs justification.

Validation: follow the flow through download, install, launch, permissions, and the first useful result. Priority: P1; P0 for broken or mismatched artifacts.

### I26 — Explain trial, pricing, and renewal accurately

Principle: help visitors understand the total commitment at the decision point.

Human behavior and evidence: S49 shows that fee salience can change purchases and spending in ticket retail. Strength: strong field evidence for that setting; disclosure here is also an informed-choice policy, not a guaranteed sales lift.

Applicability: paid software, free trials, usage limits, annual billing, and add-ons. Implementation: distinguish billing interval from equivalent monthly price, state trial conditions, and make cancellation and limits findable. Example: “Billed annually” should be visible beside the annual plan's displayed price.

Common mistake and trade-off: portraying an omitted cost as harmless simplification. Transparent information may reduce some immediate conversions; that trade-off must be acknowledged rather than concealed by a success narrative.

Validation: test users' total-cost and renewal expectations; inspect paid activation, refunds, and support issues. Priority: P0 for misleading commitments; P1 for difficult comparisons.

### I27 — Give the waitlist a truthful value proposition

Principle: explain what joining offers now and what remains uncertain.

Human behavior and evidence: this application combines I18–I20 with S19–S21; there is no reviewed universal waitlist-conversion formula. Strength: hypothesis and informed-expectation requirement.

Applicability: unreleased products, private betas, staged access. Implementation: identify the planned use case, actual product state, communication plan, selection process if known, and required next step. Example: “Get an email when beta invitations open” is more precise than “Get instant access” when no access exists.

Common mistake and trade-off: inventing a queue position, user count, launch date, or limited availability. A prototype can clarify the idea, but must not be presented as shipped functionality.

Validation: ask when and how people expect access. Measure any required verification, delivered invitations, and later activation in mature cohorts. Priority: P1; P0 for a false availability promise.

### I28 — Choose the demonstration medium by the question

Principle: use the least demanding medium that adequately communicates the relevant behavior.

Human behavior and evidence: S08–S09 and S31 support explanatory integration and segmentation; they do not establish one universally superior medium. Strength: moderate rationale, local comparison required.

Applicability: screenshots, video, diagrams, and interactive previews. Implementation: use a static result for a static-output question; use motion for a meaningful transition; use interaction when choosing or editing is the value. Example: a reversible file-cleanup preview may be better demonstrated by selecting and deselecting sample files than by a cinematic montage.

Common mistake and trade-off: adding video because a template expects it. Interaction increases development, accessibility, and performance costs; screenshots can hide causal steps.

Validation: compare comprehension and completed actions with the full page population, including people who do not activate the demo. Priority: P1 when the mechanism is unclear.

### I29 — Segment by meaning, then determine timing

Principle: organize a short demo into understandable operations before setting scene duration.

Human behavior and evidence: S31 supports segmentation in learning; S33 concerns shared gaze during videos. Strength: moderate learning evidence and preliminary transfer to promotional retention.

Applicability: 15–60 second product demonstrations and animated workflows. Implementation: define the input, one meaningful operation, result, and control. Hold readable states long enough for representative viewers to interpret them. Offer pause and replay where relevant.

Common mistake and trade-off: cutting every second or compressing three unfamiliar operations into a single unreadable transition. More time may improve understanding but lose some viewers. Test both rather than assuming completion is the sole objective.

Validation: ask viewers to reconstruct the operation and identify unsupported inferences. Measure meaningful exits separately from abandonment. Priority: P1 for unreadable or misunderstood sequences.

### I30 — Keep the story complete without autoplay or sound

Principle: essential product meaning must remain available when the video is not played.

Human behavior and evidence: G06 supplies accessibility guidance; S12 and S32 concern different viewing settings and do not establish an autoplay winner. Strength: standards and design hypothesis.

Applicability: landing-page video, social previews, muted environments, and reduced-motion preferences. Implementation: use a representative poster, concise adjacent explanation, captions for prerecorded speech, appropriate controls, and a static alternative conveying necessary visual information. Test whether description or another equivalent is needed for visual-only meaning.

Common mistake and trade-off: encoding the entire promise in a fast loop or relying on audio narration to identify the product. Duplication should clarify, not produce several competing versions of the same sentence.

Validation: inspect silent, paused, failed-load, keyboard, and assistive-technology paths. Priority: P0 if the page otherwise cannot be understood or operated; otherwise P1.

### I31 — Open with a product-relevant result

Principle: the hook should attract attention to what the product genuinely delivers.

Human behavior and evidence: S32 links emotion and viewing; S36 concerns continued content consumption. Strength: moderate adjacent evidence, opening format is a hypothesis.

Applicability: short demos and feed creatives where continuing is voluntary. Implementation: compare a direct result-first opening with a recognizable problem-first opening while keeping the claim constant. Example: show a real editable itinerary, then reveal the user's constraints and the operation that produced it.

Common mistake and trade-off: showing a spectacular unrelated scene, delaying the useful answer, or optimizing for a misleading thumbnail. An exciting output may omit effort; restore the necessary input and control context.

Validation: test in a realistic feed for stopping, and separately test product understanding and qualified action. Priority: P2 after an accurate explanatory baseline exists.

### I32 — Make mobile an equivalent explanation

Principle: preserve the important relationships and actions when the viewport changes.

Human behavior and evidence: S26 and S34 motivate accessible operation and reader-sensitive evaluation; G06 provides reflow guidance. Strength: usability rationale, product-specific mobile design remains a hypothesis.

Applicability: all responsive pages, especially large screenshots and multi-column comparisons. Implementation: recompose the example rather than merely shrinking it; keep each caption with its image and price with its conditions. Example: stack input and output with a short repeated identifier so the relation survives without tiny text.

Common mistake and trade-off: preserving a desktop composition at the cost of legibility, or hiding essential comparison details on mobile. A mobile-specific crop must not create a different product claim.

Validation: test actual text sizes, zoom, orientation, touch, slow devices, and reading order. Priority: P0 when mobile users cannot understand or act; otherwise P1.

### I33 — Choose readable typography, not a mythical best font

Principle: evaluate the actual content with representative readers and devices.

Human behavior and evidence: S34 finds substantial individual variation in reading performance across fonts. Strength: moderate, with limited population coverage; no universally fastest font follows.

Applicability: dense copy, mobile screenshots, comparisons, local languages. Implementation: establish a legible body style, sensible line length, consistent heading levels, and robust fallbacks. Keep essential copy as real text. Example: enlarge the example's result labels instead of making the surrounding headline even larger.

Common mistake and trade-off: turning one study's fastest-versus-slowest comparison into a promised uplift from a chosen typeface. Larger type can increase page length; shorter lines can fragment tables. Test the complete composition.

Validation: assess reading errors, comprehension, zoom, and task success. Priority: P0 for unreadable essentials; P1 for demonstrated reading difficulty.

### I34 — Make distinctiveness carry meaning

Principle: develop visual identity from the product's material, operation, or user outcome.

Human behavior and evidence: S30 distinguishes creative design from perceptual clutter; S10–S11 concern irrelevant detail in learning. Strength: moderate adjacent support, not a ban on decoration.

Applicability: pages that look interchangeable or use generic dashboards. Implementation: choose a product-specific organizing idea. A file-cleanup page can use an inspectable file tray; a planning product can use an evolving plan. Preserve enough restraint that the useful example remains readable.

Common mistake and trade-off: deleting all personality in pursuit of minimalism, or adding gradients, cards, badges, and motion without a role. Familiar components are allowed when they solve the actual communication problem.

Validation: apply the brand-swap test, then test recognition and comprehension with users. Distinctiveness alone is not success. Priority: P2 unless decoration actively prevents understanding.

### I35 — Build accessible operation into the first version

Principle: the page and its action path must work across relevant access needs.

Human behavior and evidence: S26 reports task benefits from higher accessibility levels; G06 and G09–G11 supply implementation guidance. Strength: moderate experimental task evidence plus standards.

Applicability: every interactive page. Implementation: use semantic headings, labels, meaningful focus order, visible focus, readable contrast, non-color error cues, reflow, keyboard controls, and suitable alternatives for media. Include form errors and success notifications, not only the hero.

Common mistake and trade-off: treating an automated audit or a high contrast ratio as complete accessibility. Meeting one criterion does not guarantee task success. A new interaction may require a simpler equivalent route.

Validation: combine automated checks, manual keyboard and screen-reader checks, and sessions with relevant users. Priority: P0 for access barriers; do not postpone essential fixes until an uplift test.

### I36 — Make useful content available promptly

Principle: measure loading, responsiveness, stability, and time to useful output separately.

Human behavior and evidence: S35 concerns interaction delay; G05 reports search-delay experiments; G08 defines Web Vitals. Strength: task-specific experimental support and current operational guidance.

Applicability: heavy heroes, interactive demos, signup, and first-run processing. Implementation: prioritize the headline, meaningful image, and action; reserve media space; defer nonessential work. Example: the CTA remains usable while an optional demo loads.

Common mistake and trade-off: adding an impressive animation that prevents the offer from appearing, or treating a laboratory score as the user's experience. Rich media can earn its cost if it explains something important and has a usable fallback.

Validation: inspect field distributions and failure rates by device, alongside local outcomes. Priority: P0 for stalled actions; P1 for substantial performance barriers.

### I37 — Explain genuine waiting without inventing work

Principle: when work takes time, show truthful progress and a clear recovery path.

Human behavior and evidence: S50 tests operational transparency in simulated services and finds context-dependent value effects. Strength: moderate for studied perceptions and preferences; no case for deliberately slowing this product.

Applicability: scanning, uploads, generation, exports, and downloads. Implementation: show actual stages or honest indeterminate status, state whether leaving is safe, and provide cancellation or retry when available. Example: “Scanning the selected folder” is appropriate only while that operation is occurring.

Common mistake and trade-off: fake thinking animations, fabricated counts, or adding artificial delay to imply intelligence. Excessive status detail can distract from the result.

Validation: test what users believe is happening, whether they abandon, and whether recovery succeeds. Measure real duration as well as perceived waiting. Priority: P1 for unexplained waits; P0 for deceptive progress.

### I38 — Calibrate expectations for AI behavior

Principle: show capability, limits, and correction without implying infallibility.

Human behavior and evidence: S46 develops and evaluates human-AI interaction guidelines with practitioners. Strength: evaluated design guidance; it is not a randomized conversion study.

Applicability: generated text, recommendations, extraction, automation, and prediction. Implementation: show a representative output and a correction step; distinguish suggestions from executed actions. Example: a suggested meeting task remains editable and is not sent to another person automatically.

Common mistake and trade-off: showing a hand-selected flawless result as typical, inventing an accuracy percentage, or overwhelming the hero with technical caveats. Put relevant boundaries beside the promise and offer deeper evaluation evidence.

Validation: ask users what happens when the model is wrong and who controls the final action. Test first-use recovery. Priority: P0 for dangerous or consequential false beliefs; otherwise P1.

### I39 — Separate attention, understanding, and action

Principle: measure each outcome directly enough to answer the decision question.

Human behavior and evidence: S22–S24 address experimental interpretation; S33 and S36 illustrate distinct engagement proxies. Strength: strong methodological rationale, no guaranteed design result.

Applicability: all optimization work. Implementation: define exposure, voluntary engagement, comprehension, completed commitment, activation, and retention separately. Example: a video play event does not demonstrate that the user saw the result or understood its limitation.

Common mistake and trade-off: selecting only viewers for the main A/B analysis or using a model's aesthetic score as conversion proof. More instrumentation costs effort, so collect the smallest reliable set needed for the decision.

Validation: inspect event correctness, assignment balance, missingness, denominators, and outcome maturity before interpreting differences. Priority: P0 when a business decision rests on invalid measurement.

### I40 — Keep the promise through first use

Principle: make the post-click experience deliver the outcome the page led visitors to expect.

Human behavior and evidence: this is an operating synthesis of S21–S24, S39, and S46. Strength: strong measurement rationale, local journey hypothesis.

Applicability: every signup, trial, purchase, download, and waitlist-to-invitation flow. Implementation: specify the first useful task and its prerequisites. If setup, payment, permissions, or manual work is required, account for it in the page and onboarding. Example: a visitor promised an editable plan should reach a sample or supported input path rather than an unexplained empty dashboard.

Common mistake and trade-off: optimizing a submit click while ignoring activation, support burden, or later regret. A better-qualified audience can produce fewer leads and more value; measure the relevant trade-off.

Validation: follow assigned cohorts through mature activation and retention windows. Priority: P1; P0 for a materially broken promise.


## Part C — Communication and scriptwriting manual

### C1. Establish product truth before writing claims

Create a product record from supplied documentation, an inspected build, and verified owner answers. Mark each item verified, provisional, unknown, or contradicted. Track the source and date. A competitor's feature is not proof that this product has it. A founder's ambition is not a shipped capability. A prototype may be useful evidence of a concept if it is labeled accurately.

Required fields: product name and category; current availability; intended users and supported markets; user task; accepted inputs; operations; outputs; user controls; supported platforms and formats; permissions and data handling; price and trial terms; capacity and usage limits; prerequisites; known failure cases; support and cancellation paths; primary conversion event; first useful result; proof assets and their provenance.

For every proposed claim, create a record with exact wording, factual basis, scope, evidence asset, owner, date, and allowed placement. “Works offline” requires checking which functions work offline and which do not. “Saves hours” requires a measured task and comparator. “No credit card required” requires verification of the actual signup path. If a material claim is unresolved, omit it from production copy or request the missing fact; do not invent a reassuring answer.

Separate product limitations from page limitations. A better visual cannot repair an unsupported format or a missing capability. If the product cannot fulfill a campaign promise, change the promise or the product before optimizing the page.

### C2. Learn the visitor's situation

Recruit people with recent experience of the relevant task, including satisfied users of existing alternatives, people who abandoned similar tools, and plausible nonbuyers. Do not recruit only fans. Use neutral invitations that do not teach the desired product explanation. Record context such as language, device, familiarity, access needs, and acquisition source when it matters to the task.

Ask about a specific recent episode: “Tell me about the last time you needed to do this.” Follow with what triggered it, what they tried, what input they had, what took effort, what they worried about, what counted as a useful result, and what happened next. Ask to see an artifact or workflow when appropriate and consented. Avoid “Would you use an app that saves time?” as evidence of demand.

Ask about the existing alternative, including doing nothing. A spreadsheet, folder convention, message to a friend, or manual workaround may already be adequate. Investigate switching costs: setup, importing data, learning, trust, collaboration, price, and habit. Distinguish an interesting feature from a reason to change behavior.

Use a visitor record with: triggering situation; desired progress; existing alternative; known category vocabulary; expected input and output; cost of the problem; willingness and ability to act now; uncertainties; observed emotional language; evidence and confidence. Do not fill gaps with demographic stereotypes or an invented persona biography.

### C3. Record feelings without pretending to read minds

| Situation | Feeling hypothesis | Evidence to seek | Possible design response |
|---|---|---|---|
| New AI tool with personal files | Interest mixed with caution | Questions about upload, storage, and correction | Representative output and concrete data-flow explanation |
| Repetitive manual task | Frustration or resignation | Recent workaround, time spent, repeated failures | Demonstrate the specific operation that changes the task |
| Unfamiliar download | Concern about installation and trust | Permission questions, refusal reasons, observed errors | Platform clarity, provenance, verified first-run guidance |
| Trial with unclear billing | Suspicion or uncertainty | Incorrect renewal predictions, pricing comparisons | Visible total commitment and accurate trial conditions |
| Product in a waitlist phase | Curiosity with skepticism | Expected access timing, interest in updates | Honest status, useful preview, clear communication promise |
| Dense page on a phone | Confusion, fatigue, or simply interruption | Reading errors, zooming, context of use | Recompose essential visuals and offer direct navigation |

These are research prompts, not diagnoses. Confirm them with users. Ask “What were you considering here?” before suggesting fear or distrust. Keep self-report and behavioral observations as separate records. Resolve discrepancies by further inquiry rather than forcing a single story.

### C4. Choose the core promise and communication route

Write three statements before the headline: the visitor's task in their words; the verified transformation the product provides; and the most important reason they may not act. The core promise should connect the first two without evading the third.

A useful drafting pattern is: “[Product/category] helps [relevant person or situation] turn [input/problem] into [useful result], with [necessary control or differentiator].” This is a thinking aid, not mandatory final syntax. Split it into a concise headline, supporting sentence, and visual if the full statement is too dense.

Choose a route based on uncertainty. For a known category and high intent, lead with the differentiating result, conditions, and action. For an unfamiliar workflow, lead with a concrete example and a short mechanism. For a skeptical audience, provide the relevant proof early. For a waitlist, establish the concept and actual availability before collecting an email. For a complex product, let informed visitors jump to technical material while newcomers see a small complete example.

Write a message ladder: essential first takeaway; supporting mechanism; evidence; important boundary; next commitment. Then decide which parts belong together. Do not equate the ladder with five required sections. A simple product may communicate several in one compact block, while a complex decision may require more depth.

### C5. Write the script before arranging the UI

For every section complete the following record. If its visitor question duplicates another section, merge it or justify the repetition. If it has no evidence or communication role, remove it from the baseline.

| Field | Required content |
|---|---|
| Section ID and role | A stable identifier and the visitor decision it supports |
| Visitor question | A concrete question, not “needs engagement” |
| Prior state | What the visitor already knows and what remains uncertain |
| Intended takeaway | One accurate statement they should be able to express |
| Exact copy | Headline, body, labels, conditions, CTA text where appropriate |
| Visual specification | The actual input, state, operation, output, annotations and proof |
| Expected response | Understanding, justified confidence, useful exploration, or action |
| Sequence rationale | Why it comes here and what information it depends on |
| Next route | Natural continuation plus any direct route for informed visitors |
| Responsive behavior | What is reordered, recropped, or made available on small screens |
| Evidence and assumptions | Applicable insight IDs, source IDs, factual basis, uncertainty |
| Validation | An observable test and a result that would falsify the design rationale |

Example script fragment for a fictional document tool: question, “Will I be able to check the extracted numbers?” Takeaway, “The extracted table remains linked to the original document.” Headline, “Check every number against its source.” Body, “Select a cell to see the matching line in your document.” Visual, one source excerpt and one selected table cell connected by a stable highlight. The interaction must work with keyboard focus and have a static equivalent. This section belongs immediately after extraction is demonstrated because trust in the result is the next uncertainty. Its local test is whether users can find and correct a deliberately wrong sample cell.

### C6. Copy editing as a comprehension test

Read only the headings and CTA labels. Can someone recover the product, useful result, evidence, and next step? Then read the supporting copy for scope and conditions. Replace unsupported superlatives with specifics. Expand unfamiliar abbreviations. Remove repeated claims that add no new evidence. Keep necessary detail even when it makes the page longer.

Use distinct language for capability, outcome, and aspiration. “Exports a CSV” is a capability. “Move the edited list into your spreadsheet” is a use outcome. “Spend less time organizing work” is a broader promise that may need user evidence. Do not silently move from the first to a guaranteed version of the third.

Avoid claims such as instant, effortless, autonomous, private, unlimited, and accurate unless their scope is established. Prefer wording that names the real control: preview, edit, choose, confirm, undo, or export, when those actions exist. Express emotional benefits through a credible experience rather than pressure.

Use analogies carefully. “A personal librarian for your files” may explain retrieval but imply human judgment, perfect memory, or privacy guarantees. Follow the analogy with the literal operation and test what people infer. Remove it if the metaphor produces more wrong predictions than useful understanding.

### C7. Claim-to-proof and objection records

Maintain a claim ledger with one row per meaningful promise. Link the public claim to evidence and implementation, not just a paper about persuasion. Research can support a presentation strategy; it cannot verify that the product itself works.

Maintain an objection ledger separately: observed question; frequency within the research sample; seriousness; affected audience; plausible cause; available answer; placement; evidence needed; test. Do not present qualitative frequency from a small sample as market prevalence. Address the largest consequential uncertainty first, not the objection that is easiest to answer.

A testimonial answers a person's experience, not an independent security audit. A benchmark answers a defined performance task, not overall ease of adoption. A working demo answers whether one supported workflow can occur, not whether it works on every user's data. Match proof to scope and make the remaining uncertainty explicit.

### C8. Use a task story only when it helps

A useful software story can be a concrete use occasion: the visitor starts with a recognizable problem, performs a meaningful operation, sees a result, and decides what to do next. It does not require drama, a villain, or a long founder narrative. S47 reviews narrative transportation, but it does not establish that this structure beats a direct explanation for software acquisition.

When writing a story, preserve the visitor's agency and the actual work required. Show the starting condition, action, change, and boundary. Compare it with a concise result-and-proof version when audience intent makes either plausible. An informed visitor may prefer a direct compatibility answer. Test relevance, reconstruction, and action; do not treat emotional absorption as understanding.

## Part D — UI/UX implementation manual

### D1. Transform the script into a purposeful interface

Allocate a component only after assigning a communication or interaction role. The role can be identification, explanation, proof, comparison, objection resolution, navigation, action, or brand expression. Brand expression is legitimate, but it should be intentional and checked against comprehension and operation.

A feature grid is appropriate when parallel independent capabilities must be compared. It is weak when a causal workflow needs to unfold. A timeline is useful for a sequence but misleading when users can act in any order. Tabs may help switch contexts but can hide important distinctions. Accordions suit optional detail; critical price or permission conditions should not depend on opening an obscure item.

Give each section a short acceptance statement: “A visitor can identify which file formats are accepted,” or “A visitor can predict the commitment after clicking.” If a screenshot looks polished but cannot satisfy its statement, revise the content or structure before adjusting decorative details.

### D2. First-screen requirements

The first view should make the product's purpose, a relevant result, and the available next step discoverable. Include essential conditions where omission would create a materially wrong expectation. This does not mean everything must fit above one universal fold. Device size, text enlargement, language, and browser UI change the visible area.

Use a concise explanatory headline and an authentic example whose content is readable at the intended size. If the product is unfamiliar, show input and outcome together or a short operation. If visitors already know the category, emphasize the difference that matters. A CTA can be available immediately without demanding that everyone use it before evaluating the product.

On a narrow screen, preserve semantic order: purpose, explanation, representative evidence, action and conditions as appropriate to the script. An image can come earlier if it provides the clearest explanation, but it must not detach a condition from the action it qualifies. Test the assembled first view rather than isolated components.

### D3. Layout, density, and page length

Use spacing to express grouping. Maintain a consistent reading path while allowing direct jumps to key answers. Keep comparison attributes aligned and label units. Repeat a CTA where a visitor has just gained enough information to decide, not at an arbitrary interval. A repeated CTA should represent the same commitment unless explicitly labeled otherwise.

Choose length from the decisions that need support. Add a section when it resolves a meaningful new uncertainty. Delete or merge sections that repeat the same benefit without additional evidence. A long page can be efficient if visitors can find relevant information directly; a short page can be costly if it hides prerequisites.

For sticky headers or bottom actions, test overlap with content, focus, keyboards, consent notices, and zoom. Preserve ordinary scrolling and back navigation. Avoid scroll locking or mandatory animation sequences unless the interaction truly needs them and an equivalent accessible route exists.

### D4. Typography, color, and visual identity

Establish a small, consistent type hierarchy and verify it with real copy. A starting body size around 16–18 CSS pixels and a moderate reading measure can be useful design defaults, but they are not proven conversion thresholds or universal accessibility guarantees. Adjust for the font, script, content, device, and testing. Avoid rigid text containers that clip when content grows.

Use accent color to distinguish an action or meaningful state, with text or shape supporting the distinction. There is no universally best conversion color. Preserve contrast in normal, hover, focus, selected, error, and disabled states. Do not use low contrast to make important details visually convenient.

Develop identity from the product's actual substance: a characteristic output, a meaningful transformation, a distinctive but readable visual metaphor, or a voice grounded in the audience. A generic gradient or card layout is not forbidden; it must earn its role. A page should retain a recognizable product idea when its logo and accent color are removed.

### D5. Select the demonstration format deliberately

| Format | Best question to answer | Implementation and risk |
|---|---|---|
| Static screenshot | What does the result look like? | Crop legibly; preserve context; avoid implying interaction |
| Annotated comparison | What changed and why is it useful? | Match before and after conditions; label units and inputs |
| Step walkthrough | How does the task proceed? | Show coherent steps with persistent context and direct navigation |
| Short video | What meaningful transformation occurs over time? | Readable holds, controls, captions, poster and static explanation |
| Interactive preview | What can I choose, change, or inspect? | Use safe sample data, clear reset, keyboard support, truthful scope |
| Concept diagram | What relationship is otherwise invisible? | Keep it literal and labeled; avoid suggesting nonexistent architecture |
| Live product trial | Will it work on my input? | Explain permissions, cost, limits, failure states and data handling first |

Specify the exact sample input, actual output, operation, cursor or focus behavior, label positions, and endpoint. For a mockup, label its status in a way visitors can notice. For a real recording, preserve enough context to avoid implying automatic steps that were performed manually. If waiting is compressed, say so when the timing could influence the decision.

### D6. A reusable short-demo production specification

Define the viewer, traffic setting, desired takeaway, supported input, visible operation, result, necessary limitation, and next action. Produce a shot list with scene purpose, screen state, exact overlay, narration if used, minimum readable hold determined by testing, transition, accessible alternative, and failure risk.

For a 30-second draft, a plausible editorial allocation is 0–4 seconds for a recognizable result, 4–9 for input and context, 9–19 for one operation, 19–25 for a useful result or correction, and 25–30 for the next action. These are production starting points, not scientific attention thresholds. Test a direct explanatory opening against a problem-first opening if that uncertainty matters.

For 15 seconds, reduce claims rather than speed up essential reading. For 45–60 seconds, add a meaningful decision, boundary, or proof rather than a feature montage. Do not make the final CTA the only opportunity to act. Offer a next step outside the player so a convinced visitor can leave the video successfully.

Choose the poster frame as carefully as the opening: it should identify the product outcome and remain legible at thumbnail size. Compare title and thumbnail together because each supplies context. A sensational image that increases clicks while lowering correct expectations is a failed promise, even if the click metric improves.

### D7. Responsive and interrupted use

Define layouts at representative narrow, medium, and wide widths, then test between them. Use content-driven breakpoints. Reflow comparisons into labeled groups without changing which price, condition, or benefit belongs together. Keep essential labels as text rather than rasterizing the page into a visual.

Test text enlargement, browser zoom, long translations, landscape orientation, an open on-screen keyboard, reduced motion, slow loading, and return after interruption. These are planned stress conditions, not assumptions about every visitor. Preserve entered data where appropriate. Make the current step and next action clear when someone returns.

For desktop-only software, the mobile page should explain compatibility and provide a legitimate continuation route. Do not imply the software runs on a phone. If offering an email link, explain that purpose and distinguish it from marketing subscription. Cross-device attribution will remain imperfect; report the limitation rather than counting every mobile departure as rejection.

### D8. Forms, authentication, and complete states

Specify persistent labels, required and optional fields, correct input types, autocomplete where appropriate, understandable format requirements, validation timing, error messages, focus behavior, duplicate submission handling, and confirmation. Preserve valid information after an error. Avoid making users guess why submission is blocked.

A complete state specification covers empty, typing, invalid, valid, submitting, success, duplicate, timeout, offline, unavailable, and rate-limited cases as applicable. Not every state needs a distinct screen, but every real outcome needs understandable feedback. A frontend animation does not establish server-side success.

For authentication, support password managers and pasting rather than requiring unnecessary memorization or transcription. WCAG 2.2's accessible-authentication criterion focuses on authentication and includes specific alternatives and exceptions; it is not a blanket prohibition on passwords. Apply its techniques thoughtfully to account creation as well. [G10]

### D9. Accessibility acceptance requirements

Use the current WCAG reference and assess the full applicable criteria. The checks below are selected implementation priorities, not a complete conformance claim. [G06]

For ordinary text, verify at least 4.5:1 contrast; qualifying large text uses 3:1, with criterion-specific exceptions. Assess non-text controls and states under the relevant separate criteria. Color cannot be the only cue. [G11]

For pointer targets, WCAG 2.2 AA includes a 24 by 24 CSS-pixel minimum with defined exceptions, including spacing and certain inline targets. Prefer larger usable targets where the task and layout support them; 44 by 44 is an enhanced criterion, not the same AA minimum. [G09]

Test semantic structure, keyboard access, visible focus, focus not obscured, reflow, labels, error identification, meaningful names, and announced status changes. Provide appropriate media alternatives and user control. Verify that hover-only content, drag interactions, or autoplay are not the sole path to essential information. Automated tools identify some issues; they cannot certify the whole experience or replace task testing.

### D10. Performance and truthful responsiveness

Current Core Web Vitals good thresholds are LCP at or below 2.5 seconds, INP at or below 200 milliseconds, and CLS at or below 0.1, assessed at the 75th percentile. These are operational experience targets, not predicted conversion lifts. Inspect field data by relevant device conditions and use lab testing to diagnose problems. [G08]

Set performance budgets around the actual page: critical content delivery, image weight, font loading, script execution, demo startup, and form response. Prioritize a usable explanation and action over an optional animation. Reserve dimensions to avoid unexpected movement. Provide a stable poster and readable copy before video becomes available.

Measure time to first useful product result separately from page loading. If generation is slow, explain actual progress and safe next steps. Do not fabricate work to increase perceived value. Ensure timeout, cancellation, and retry behavior preserve correct expectations.

### D11. Anti-template review

Run the brand-swap test: replace the name and logo. If the page still fits any unrelated software product, the message or visual may be insufficiently specific. This is an editorial diagnostic, not a validated conversion score.

Run the removal test: for each component, state what visitor question becomes harder to answer if it disappears. A legitimate answer may concern orientation, proof, action, or brand experience. If there is no useful answer, remove it from the baseline. Do not equate “decorative” with automatically harmful; evaluate competition and purpose.

Run the reality test: compare screenshots, copy, interactions, and implied speed with the actual product. Run the state test: attempt the real action and recover from an error. Run the mobile test: read the example without zooming solely to decipher a shrunken desktop UI. Run the expectation test: ask someone what will happen after the CTA.

The aim is a distinctive explanation of this product. Visual fashion is neither proof of effectiveness nor a reason to prohibit a useful pattern.

## Part F — Evaluation and optimization toolkit

### F1. A consistent diagnostic rubric

Score each dimension from 0 to 3, or mark NA with a reason. Use 0 for absent, broken, or materially misleading; 1 for present but ambiguous or incomplete; 2 for a clear implementation that passes inspection but lacks relevant user confirmation; 3 for an implementation supported by appropriate observed user or operational evidence. A 3 is contextual and expires when the product, audience, or flow changes substantially. Do not award it merely because the AI likes the result.

For each dimension, the table defines what a good implementation must demonstrate. Apply the common scale against that criterion. Record the evidence, severity, affected users, recommended correction, applicable insight, and next test. A sum may help internal triage but is not a validated predictor of conversion. Do not let a high total cancel a serious false claim or inaccessible action.

| Dimension | Criterion for a clear implementation | Relevant checks |
|---|---|---|
| Immediate product clarity | Purpose and outcome can be accurately explained | Unaided interpretation; I01, I07, I08 |
| Audience relevance | A real use occasion and fit are recognizable | Recent-task interviews; I14 |
| Value proposition | Benefit is meaningful relative to the actual alternative | Relevance and switching questions; I14, I15 |
| Product demonstration | Input, operation, result and scope are faithful and readable | Reconstruction task; I09, I28 |
| Ease of understanding | Relationships require little unnecessary inference | Misconception and memory checks; I10–I13 |
| Information sequence | Necessary answers are available when needed | Finding tasks and direct routes; I05 |
| Hierarchy and readability | Important information can be found and read | Actual-size reading and mobile tests; I03, I33 |
| Authenticity and distinction | Visuals and copy express this product accurately | Brand-swap and reality tests; I34 |
| Trust and objections | Relevant uncertainties have substantiated answers | Claim-to-proof and permission tests; I17, I18 |
| CTA clarity | Visitors can predict the next commitment | Pre-click prediction; I20, I21 |
| Conversion friction | Willing visitors complete and recover successfully | End-to-end tasks; I22–I27 |
| Mobile responsiveness | Equivalent meaning and action survive smaller viewports | Device, zoom, keyboard and interruption; I32 |
| Accessibility | Applicable access requirements and task paths are assessed | Automated and manual checks; I35 |
| Performance | Critical content and actions respond reliably | Field and lab measurements; I36, I37 |
| Evidence alignment | Claims, rules and uncertainty are traceable | Source and decision audit; I39, I40 |

Stop shipment for unresolved material falsehoods, broken primary actions, or known essential access barriers. Other weaknesses should enter a prioritized backlog. This is a release policy, not a conclusion that every item has equal commercial impact.

### F2. Run a comprehension session

Write the answer key before showing the page. Define the correct purpose, audience or use occasion, input, operation, output, important limitation, and expected next step. Score each as 0 wrong or absent, 1 partly correct, or 2 accurate enough for the decision. Record critical misconceptions separately so they cannot disappear inside an average.

Recruit participants who plausibly face the task. A small initial round can identify problems; it does not establish prevalence or a precise conversion rate. Include unfamiliar visitors and relevant access or language needs. If comparing versions, use separate participants or counterbalance exposure, and recognize that prior viewing can teach the answer.

Begin with a brief exposure, for example five seconds, then hide the page. Ask: “What do you think this is?” “What would you use it for?” “What result would you get?” “What do you expect after the main button?” Five seconds is an experimental condition, not a universal human attention span. Avoid correcting answers during the first assessment.

Then allow self-paced examination and ask the same core questions plus a new-example prediction. Ask how confident the person is separately from accuracy. Follow with a realistic task such as finding compatibility, assessing the trial cost, or reaching a sample result. Use retrospective questions to understand what they considered. Think-aloud is useful for diagnosis but can alter natural speed and attention; do not treat that session as an unmodified browsing measurement.

Report actual responses, errors, task completion, and the conditions of the test. Include contradictory observations. A participant repeating a headline is weaker evidence than correctly predicting what the software will do with a different input.

### F3. Test the full commitment path

Ask willing participants to carry out the intended action in a safe test environment. Observe fields, errors, permissions, payment expectations, download handling, confirmation, onboarding, and the first meaningful task. Do not collect real sensitive data merely to make the test realistic; use suitable sample data and consented observation.

Test at least the known high-risk states: a malformed entry, lost connection, duplicate submission, unavailable service, interrupted process, and return to the page. Confirm that success means the server accepted the action or the expected artifact exists, rather than a local visual state changing.

For download products, distinguish click, transfer completion where observable, installation, first launch, and first useful result. Browser analytics may not reliably observe every stage. Use product telemetry only with appropriate transparency and document gaps. For a waitlist, verify confirmation and later invitation behavior without inventing a launch schedule.

### F4. Test stopping and viewing in their real context

A forced full-screen demo can test understanding but cannot establish whether it would stop a feed scroll. For stopping, place the creative among realistic alternatives with a defined opportunity to see it. Record eligible exposure, deliberate selection or stopping, continued viewing, and subsequent explanation. Define a stopping threshold operationally and check whether conclusions change under reasonable alternatives.

For video, record content version, poster, title, exposure, play origin, audible state, buffering, visible duration, seeking, ending, and next action. Distinguish autoplay from deliberate play and avoid treating background playback as attention. Completion percentage mechanically favors shorter videos; compare absolute meaningful exposure, comprehension, and product action as well.

Analyze title and thumbnail jointly. A high-performing combination may owe its performance to audience, distribution, topic, or prior familiarity rather than the image alone. When possible, randomize variants within comparable audiences and delivery conditions. Do not infer psychological motives directly from a retention curve.

### F5. Analytics event contract

| Event | Definition and trigger | Important boundary |
|---|---|---|
| experiment_assigned | Eligible visitor assigned before treatment delivery | Preserve assignment across visits where feasible |
| page_delivery | Page request or delivery outcome | Record failures; do not silently remove slow users |
| key_content_rendered | Defined essential content became usable | Not evidence it was read |
| section_exposed | Predefined visibility threshold and duration | Diagnostic convention, not proof of attention |
| cta_activated | Named action activated with its location | Not a completed signup or download |
| form_started | Meaningful first input under a specified definition | Avoid capturing field contents |
| form_error | Validated error category and state | No raw email, password, or personal text in logs |
| submission_accepted | Server-confirmed successful submission | Deduplicate retries and duplicates |
| verification_completed | Actual required verification succeeded | Only applicable if the flow requires it |
| demo_play | Start with user-initiated or autoplay flag | Keep visibility, buffering and sound separate |
| demo_progress | Defined milestones or visible watch duration | Seeking and background playback can distort it |
| product_activated | Predeclared first meaningful useful behavior | Not automatically a login or dashboard view |
| retained_use | Meaningful repeat use in a mature window | Compare cohorts with equal opportunity |
| cancellation_or_regret | Relevant cancellation, refund, complaint or unsubscribe | Interpret by product and observation window |

Common fields: event ID, pseudonymous visitor or assignment ID as appropriate, timestamp, page and content version, experiment and variant, traffic source, device class, locale, and error category. Use data minimization, documented retention, appropriate consent, and access controls. Mask session-replay fields and avoid recording sensitive inputs. The analytics plan is a technical design, not legal advice or a claim of regulatory compliance.

Primary conversion rate is completed intended actions divided by eligible assigned visitors in the defined window. Activation per assigned visitor measures downstream contribution without conditioning on who signed up. Activation among signups is a useful diagnostic but may change because the design changes the people who enroll. Likewise, a form completion rate among starters is not the full-page conversion rate.

### F6. A disciplined experiment record

Before launch record the observed problem, audience, proposed change, mechanism, evidence IDs, primary metric, guardrails, minimum useful effect, assignment unit, eligibility, duration or stopping rule, sample requirement, exclusion rules, and analysis plan. State what counts as support, harm, or an inconclusive result. Randomize at a unit that limits contamination and account for repeated visitors or shared accounts.

Run an instrumentation check before interpreting effects. Check assignment balance, duplicate events, missing data, render failures, bot filtering rules, and consistent metric definitions. Investigate sample-ratio mismatch rather than explaining it away. Keep versions stable unless the experiment design explicitly accommodates change. Document outages and concurrent campaigns. [S22, S23]

For ordinary fixed-horizon tests, do not stop when a favorable p-value first appears. Use a predeclared valid sequential method if continuous monitoring is required. Report absolute and relative differences with uncertainty, not a winner label alone. Adjust for multiple comparisons where relevant. Separate exploratory segments from prespecified analyses. Look for novelty and learning effects and allow downstream windows to mature. [S24]

Illustration: moving a 5% conversion rate to 6% is one percentage point absolute and 20% relative. Under equal allocation, independent visitor outcomes, two-sided 5% significance and 80% power, a conventional normal approximation requires about 8,200 visitors per variant. This is an illustrative calculation, not a prescription. At 200 eligible visitors per day across both arms, enrollment alone is about 82 days. Other baselines, dependencies, methods, and useful effects change the requirement.

If traffic is insufficient, use realistic user research to repair misunderstandings and interaction failures, improve measurement, and test larger meaningful concepts when justified. Do not declare a tiny sample a proof of conversion. Bayesian analysis also requires justified assumptions and enough information; it does not create missing data.

### F7. Prioritize by the observed barrier

| Observed pattern | Plausible explanations | Useful next action |
|---|---|---|
| Visitors misidentify the product | Vague category, misleading visual, campaign mismatch | I01–I09; unaided interpretation |
| They understand but see little value | Weak need, adequate alternative, wrong audience | Recent-task discovery; I14–I15 |
| They see value but doubt the claim | Missing evidence, unrepresentative demo, trust concern | Claim-specific proof test; I17–I18 |
| They hesitate over commitment | Cost, permissions, availability, setup uncertainty | Expectation test; I20, I25–I27 |
| They intend to act but fail | Form, platform, accessibility or performance barrier | End-to-end task inspection; I22–I24, I35–I37 |
| Signups rise while activation falls | Wrong expectations, changed audience mix, onboarding failure | Assigned-cohort analysis; I38–I40 |
| Watch time rises without understanding | Attractive distraction, unreadable states, weak explanation | Reconstruction test; I28–I31 |

Use a transparent qualitative ranking: severity of the observed problem, reach, confidence that the proposed change addresses it, potential value, implementation cost, and reversibility. A numeric impact-confidence-effort score can organize discussion, but invented precision does not make it scientific. Fix known blockers before comparing decorative variants.

### F8. Analyze a dataset of pages, thumbnails, and outcomes

A screenshot plus title and total views cannot identify click propensity because exposure is missing. A page screenshot plus raw signups cannot identify conversion without eligible visits. Collect exposure, time period, traffic source, targeting, offer, product, brand, device, creative version, outcomes, and attribution rules. Preserve missingness rather than guessing denominators.

Create a coding guide for observable features: category clarity, headline specificity, visible output, readable labels, input–output relationship, proof proximity, CTA commitments, motion, fields, price clarity, and availability. Use independent coding on a subset, resolve disagreements, and retain LLM model and prompt versions if models assist. Hide outcomes from coders when feasible.

Begin with descriptive analysis and simple baselines. Account for product, brand, campaign, and repeated-observation clustering. Use holdouts by product or time rather than splitting near-duplicate screenshots across train and test. Avoid treating downstream scrolling or video watching as pre-treatment confounders. Predictive feature importance and saliency maps are not causal explanations.

Use observational patterns to generate hypotheses, controlled experiments to estimate changes, and user observation to understand plausible mechanisms. Preserve negative and inconclusive results. The useful output is a conditional rule with context and uncertainty, not a universal list of visual ingredients that supposedly causes success.

### F9. Update the knowledge base after testing

Store the decision ID, page version, audience, traffic, product state, experiment dates, hypothesis, evidence, metric definitions, actual result, uncertainty, quality checks, and implementation decision. Mark each rule research-informed, locally supported, contradicted, or unresolved. Record what changed and what remains unknown.

Do not convert one local win into a global rule. Recheck it when the offer, audience, acquisition mix, product capability, or platform changes. Preserve failures so the AI does not repeatedly propose a tactic already tested under the same conditions. Re-evaluate source currency for technical standards and implementation guidance before a new release.

### G1. Desktop download: FolderPilot

Product brief: a Windows 11 application that scans a user-selected folder for byte-identical duplicate files. It previews candidates and only moves selected files to the Recycle Bin after confirmation. Scanning is local. It does not identify visually similar photos. The fictional release is free, requires no account, and is available now.

Visitor and goal: someone running out of storage after copying folders between drives. Their existing alternative is manual comparison or buying more storage. They may hope to recover space but worry about losing the wrong file. The goal is successful first use of the preview after installation; download initiation is an intermediate metric. A mobile visitor cannot complete the Windows task immediately.

Communication strategy: lead with a controlled cleanup outcome, then make the operation and boundary inspectable. Use a file tray as the organizing visual idea. Do not lead with a generic productivity slogan or a full dashboard. Relevant cards: I07, I09, I18, I25, I32, I40.

Section FP1 — Orientation and immediate action. Visitor question: “Can this help me clear duplicates safely?” Headline: “Find identical copies. Choose what to remove.” Body: “FolderPilot scans a folder on your Windows PC and shows matching files before you change anything.” CTA: “Download for Windows 11.” Adjacent conditions: “Free. No account. Local scanning.” Secondary route: “See the preview.” Visual: a real folder selection followed by three identical-copy groups, with a retained copy clearly distinguished. The first takeaway is controlled inspection, not automatic deletion.

Section FP2 — Demonstrate the safeguard. Question: “What happens before a file is removed?” Headline: “Review every group before confirming.” Body: “Compare names and locations, change the selection, and confirm the files you want moved to the Recycle Bin.” Visual: show one group, deselect a candidate, then display the confirmation summary. Do not use a delete animation that bypasses the confirmation. This section follows the hero because control is the strongest hypothesized concern.

Section FP3 — Clarify fit and privacy. Question: “Which files match, and do they leave my PC?” Headline: “Identical files, checked on your computer.” Body: “The scan compares byte-identical files in the folder you choose. Similar-looking photos with different file contents are not treated as duplicates. File contents are not uploaded for scanning.” Visual: two identical-copy examples beside one explicitly nonmatching edited photo. This prevents an attractive but incorrect inference about visual similarity.

Section FP4 — Installation and next step. Question: “What do I do after downloading?” Headline: “Open FolderPilot and choose a folder to preview.” Body: “Install the Windows app, choose a folder, and review the scan before confirming any changes.” CTA: “Download for Windows 11.” Provide actual publisher and release information only once verified. On mobile, replace the primary platform action with clear compatibility and an optional “Send me the download link” flow whose email purpose is explicit.

Implementation: use readable filenames, generous selectable rows, and a keyboard-operable preview. Mobile shows one duplicate group at a time without hiding the matching rule. Define download failure, unsupported platform, scan progress, no duplicates, permission denied, and canceled confirmation states. A 30-second demonstration can show the selected folder, one duplicate group, a changed selection, and the result; show a persistent caption that it is a sample folder.

Review and test plan: test whether visitors mistakenly expect similar-photo detection, automatic deletion, or cloud upload. Observe installation and the first preview on a supported device. Compare a control-focused hero with a space-recovery hero while preserving identical factual boundaries. Track assigned-visitor activation, wrong expectations, scan errors, and cancellation behavior. A decrease in raw download clicks would not automatically defeat a version that substantially reduces wrong-platform installs and improves useful first runs.

### G2. Unfamiliar AI workflow: ThreadPlan

Users review and export the list; nothing is automatically assigned or sent. The fictional service processes text on a server and offers a sample preview before registration. Data retention terms must be verified before launch copy is finalized.

Visitor and goal: an individual who manually turns notes into tasks. They may assume the tool records meetings or automatically manages a team; both assumptions would be wrong. They may want relief from tedious organization while doubting whether AI will invent work. The conversion goal is account creation followed by review and export of a supported list.

Communication strategy: show the transformation and correction in one example. Use the correspondence between source notes and tasks as the visual identity. Do not hide the correction step to make the AI appear magical. Relevant cards: I07–I11, I18, I24, I28, I38.

Section TP1 — Concrete explanation. Headline: “Turn meeting notes into a task list you can check.” Body: “Paste your notes, review suggested tasks against their source sentences, and export the list you approve.” Primary CTA: “Try the sample.” Secondary CTA: “Create an account.” Visual: a short source paragraph and a readable three-task output, with one source-to-task relationship highlighted. The sample must be labeled as sample data.

Section TP2 — Correctness and control. Question: “What if a suggestion is wrong?” Headline: “Keep the useful suggestions. Edit the rest.” Body: “Select a task to see the source sentence. Change its wording or remove it before export.” Visual: a user corrects one suggestion, then the edited task remains visibly linked to the relevant source. Do not imply that every source relation proves the suggestion is correct. Provide a static equivalent of the edit.

Section TP3 — Boundaries and data. Question: “Does it record or contact anyone?” Headline: “You provide the notes. You approve the output.” Body: “ThreadPlan works from text you paste. It does not record meetings, send messages, or assign tasks automatically.” Add a short, verified statement of server processing and data retention beside the upload or paste commitment. If retention remains unknown, the design specification must block production publication of a privacy promise rather than fill it with “secure and private.”

Section TP4 — Action and first result. Headline: “Start with one meeting.” Body: “Create an account to work with your own notes, then review your first suggested list.” CTA: “Create an account.” The onboarding screen should offer a sample or a clearly explained paste field, not a blank dashboard. State actual price and usage conditions when available.

Implementation: synchronize source highlights and task selection without requiring hover; support keyboard focus and text selection. On mobile, show the selected task with its source immediately beneath it. The interactive sample has reset, loading, no result, invalid input, and error states. A result-first demo can begin with the editable list, then show the source and correction. Any recording must represent actual output rather than a fictional flawless generation.

Review and test plan: ask users to explain input, output, review responsibility, and automatic actions. Use a new note containing ambiguity to test whether they expect perfect interpretation. Compare the source-linked demonstration with a simpler static result; measure reconstruction and activation per assigned visitor. Test data-flow expectations before asking for personal notes. Do not optimize for confidence if accuracy of expectations declines.

Headline: “Hear when you can try it.” Form label: “Email address.” Button: “Join the waitlist.” Supporting copy: “We will use this address for testing invitations and availability updates. Timing and pricing are not yet confirmed.” Confirmation: “You are on the waitlist. We will email when invitations are available.” Include any actual required verification step explicitly and do not display a fabricated queue position.

Implementation: a short page may suffice because there is limited verified detail. Keep the concept and availability label together at every viewport. Do not add testimonials, user counts, pricing cards, or urgency just to fill a conventional layout. Provide a useful confirmation and a genuine way to change communication preferences. Ask only fields justified by the invitation process.

Review and test plan: ask when people expect access, whether they expect to pay now, and which capabilities they believe already exist. Interview recent group planners before assuming the coordination problem is important. Test concept comprehension and email-flow completion. If comparing early-access language with update-focused language, keep availability equally truthful. Measure later cohorts with equivalent invitation opportunity; raw address collection is not proof of product-market fit.

### G4. Consumer SaaS free trial: StudyBoard

Product brief: a web application for turning a student's own study topics into a manually editable weekly plan and tracking completed sessions. The fictional trial lasts 14 days, requires no card, and ends without automatic charging. The paid price is an unresolved product fact, so production pricing copy cannot yet be completed. The product does not guarantee grades or automatically know a course syllabus.

Visitor and goal: a student balancing assignments and available study time. The alternative may be a calendar or a paper checklist. They may want control and a realistic plan rather than another system to maintain. The goal is trial signup followed by creation of a plan and completion of a first study session, with later repeat use tracked separately.

Communication strategy: show how topics become a plan that can be adjusted when life changes. Lead with the useful planning task, then address setup and trial commitment. Relevant cards: I10, I14, I15, I20–I22, I26, I40.

Section SB1 — Recognizable result. Headline: “Make a study plan that fits the week you actually have.” Body: “Add your topics and available time, arrange the sessions, and adjust your plan as the week changes.” CTA: “Start the 14-day trial.” Adjacent copy: “No card required. The trial ends without an automatic charge.” Visual: a simple week with three study sessions and one open interval. Do not imply the tool improves grades.

Section SB2 — Setup and control. Question: “How much work is this to start?” Headline: “Begin with the topics already on your list.” Body: “Add a topic, choose a session, and move it when your schedule changes.” Visual: a real small workflow from one topic to one scheduled session, with keyboard-accessible editing as well as any drag interaction. If setup takes manual effort, show it rather than cutting directly to a completed plan.

Section SB3 — Practical continuation. Question: “Will I keep using it when I miss a session?” Headline: “Adjust the plan without starting again.” Body: “Move an unfinished session and keep the rest of your week.” Visual: a missed session moved to a later slot without a punitive red streak or a guilt message. This emotional tone is a hypothesis about supporting control and should be tested with students.

Section SB4 — Trial and pricing decision. Headline: “Try it with one real week.” Body: “Use the 14-day trial to build a plan and track your study sessions.” CTA: “Start the 14-day trial.” Provide the verified paid price and feature limits before publishing the purchase path; the design must not imply that the product stays free indefinitely. A production blocker remains until those facts are settled.

Implementation: use the plan itself as the main demonstration. A feature grid is unnecessary unless users need to compare distinct capabilities. On mobile, a readable day list may communicate more than a shrunken seven-column calendar. The signup should lead to a small first task and a sample option. Define empty plan, no available time, invalid schedule, saved edit, and trial-ended states.

Review and test plan: test whether users understand manual setup, trial expiry, and paid continuation. Compare a control-focused promise with a task-organization promise using equivalent facts. Measure trial signup, first plan, first session, mature repeat use, and support questions. Avoid interpreting more account creation as success if students immediately abandon the planning work.

### G5. Technically complex product: FlowBridge

Product brief: a tool for technically comfortable consumers and independent creators to map fields from a CSV export into another application's import format. It previews transformations and exports a new file; it does not connect directly to accounts or synchronize data continuously. A sample sandbox is available. Real file support and pricing must be specified before public release.

Visitor and goal: someone moving a reading list, personal catalog, or project archive between applications. Their mental model may be “automatic migration,” which would overstate the tool. They may care about preserving information, supported fields, and reversibility more than a broad productivity benefit. The goal is a successful supported sample mapping, followed by a qualified account or purchase action when terms are known.

Communication strategy: lead with a small concrete mapping, then offer technical details for informed visitors. The page must prevent the false inference of continuous synchronization. Relevant cards: I07, I10–I12, I17, I21, I28, I38.

Section FB1 — Explain the transformation. Headline: “Reshape a CSV export for the next app.” Body: “Map the fields, preview the changes, and export a new file in a supported import format.” Primary CTA: “Try the sample mapping.” Secondary route: “See supported formats.” Visual: three source columns mapped to three destination fields, with one renamed column and one transformed date. Keep values readable and units explicit.

Section FB2 — Inspect the mechanism. Question: “What changes, and can I check it?” Headline: “See each change before you export.” Body: “Inspect the source value, transformation rule, and destination value in one preview.” Visual: a selected row with all three related states visible. The demonstration includes an unsupported value and explains how it is flagged; do not show only ideal data.

Section FB3 — Compatibility and boundaries. Question: “Will it work with my files?” Headline: “Check the format before moving your data.” Body: “FlowBridge works with supported CSV structures and produces a new export. It does not sign into your accounts or keep applications synchronized.” Provide a verified format list and a small downloadable sample when available. A compatibility matrix has a real comparison role here; it is not decorative density.

Section FB4 — Technical depth. Question: “Can I understand the mapping rules?” Headline: “Inspect the rules behind the preview.” Body: “Review field names, accepted values, and transformation behavior before using your own file.” Link to actual documentation. Let experienced visitors reach this material directly without forcing them through the introductory story.

Section FB5 — Appropriate next action. Headline: “Try one mapping before committing your archive.” CTA: “Open the sample sandbox.” Explain that the sample contains fictional data and changes no accounts. When an account or payment is later required, disclose the verified terms before that transition. Do not turn a sample CTA into an undisclosed signup gate.

Implementation: use aligned columns on wide screens and linked labeled cards on mobile. Preserve source and destination associations during selection. Include invalid file, unsupported field, partial conversion, preview, export, and reset states. Provide a table alternative for diagrams and keyboard access to the sample. Use a still explanation as fallback if the sandbox fails.

Review and test plan: test whether users correctly distinguish file conversion from account migration and synchronization. Ask them to predict the outcome of a new row with an invalid date. Observe whether novices need more explanation while experienced visitors seek the schema. Compare a static worked example with the interactive sandbox using comprehension, task completion, performance, and later qualified action. More sandbox interaction alone does not prove a better acquisition experience.

## Evidence register and research provenance

Source IDs S01–S36 retain the identifiers and bibliographic access notes from the earlier research report. S37–S51 are the additional research entries for this book. G entries are guidance, standards, or industry evidence and are not counted as peer-reviewed experiments. Research access labels refer to material inspected, not a claim that every statistical analysis or supplement was independently audited.

Numeric findings are retained only when verified. NR means not verified in the inspected material. The absence of an effect estimate in an entry does not imply that the original paper reports none. Where participant N or an uncertainty interval is unavailable, the AI must not invent it. Each associated insight card supplies a practical application and local validation route. No source in this register proves a universal software landing-page conversion lift.

Davis, F. D. (1989). Perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and user acceptance of information technology. MIS Quarterly, 13(3), 319–340. DOI: 10.2307/249008.

Publisher-indexed abstract inspected; direct page opening was blocked. Two scale-development and validation studies covered 152 users and four applications. ### S43 Decision framing

Tversky, A., and Kahneman, D. (1981). The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice. Science, 211(4481), 453–458. DOI: 10.1126/science.7455683.

Publisher abstract inspected. Choice problems involving monetary outcomes and lives demonstrate preference reversals under different formulations; participant N and detailed estimates NR here. ### S44 Loss-aversion estimates

Brown, A. L., Imai, T., Vieider, F. M., and Camerer, C. F. (2024). Meta-analysis of empirical estimates of loss aversion. Journal of Economic Literature, 62(2), 485–516. DOI: 10.1257/jel.20221698.

Publisher abstract inspected. The analysis synthesizes 607 estimates from 150 articles, covering estimates reported from 1992–2017. ### S48 Counterevidence to a simple depletion account

Hagger, M. S., and colleagues (2016). A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect. Perspectives on Psychological Science, 11(4), 546–573. DOI: 10.1177/1745691616652873.

Publisher abstract and protocol summary inspected. Twenty-three laboratories, total N = 2,141, replicated a specified sequential-task protocol. The pooled effect was d = 0.04, 95% CI [−0.07, 0.15]. ## Research gaps, governance, and final handoff

### What the book does not establish

The review does not establish a universal highest-converting layout, color, font, word count, number of CTAs, hero duration, or emotional formula. Much of the evidence concerns perception, learning, task performance, intentions, or engagement rather than consumer software activation. Historical populations, English-language samples, publication bias, repeated observations, and overlapping meta-analyses limit generalization. Abstract-level access restricts methodological appraisal. Technical standards and guidance can change.

Product-market fit, willingness to pay, retention, cultural meaning, language, acquisition algorithms, and the quality of a particular product still require direct evidence. Different visitor goals may justify different pages. A polished prototype and a self-review do not constitute a real-world test. This manual supplies a disciplined way to make and improve design decisions, not certainty that a given page will work.

### Next research questions when applying the book

First determine what is actually uncertain: the task's importance, category understanding, result credibility, willingness to switch, permission concern, operational friction, or ability to reach value. Use recent-task interviews for relevance, comprehension sessions for mental models, observed tasks for interaction, and controlled experiments for causal comparisons where traffic permits.

Investigate language and cultural interpretation with the intended audience rather than translating emotional claims literally. Verify willingness to pay through appropriately designed offer research and real behavior; stated interest alone is insufficient. Investigate long-term value with mature cohorts. Search for new external evidence only when it can materially change an unresolved decision.

### Release record

Before handing a design to implementation, retain the verified product facts, target visitor and traffic assumptions, metric definitions, complete communication script, selected insight cards, exact visuals and provenance, responsive interaction states, proof ledger, accessibility and performance checks, known unresolved issues, and prioritized validation plan.

After implementation, inspect the actual page and complete action flow. Record defects and corrections. After launch, preserve exposure and outcome definitions and evaluate mature results. Update local rules with context instead of rewriting the science to fit a preferred design. The repeatable process is: understand the task, make a truthful promise, demonstrate it clearly, enable action, observe the result, and revise the decision that the evidence challenges.
